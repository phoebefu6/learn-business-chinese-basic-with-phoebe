The tone trainer

Sessions 1 and 2 said a tone is a shape measured against your own voice. Tonight that claim is put to work by a real pitch tracker running in this page. It listens, finds your pitch a hundred times a second, cuts the stream into syllables, and compares each syllable's shape with the four tones. Before you use it on yourself, the page runs it on four synthetic voices, high, low, narrow and wide, with three ways of scoring. One way gets a lower voice right 6 times in 23. Another gets every voice right 23 times in 23. The difference is entirely in the arithmetic, and it is the reason "your pitch is too low" is never useful feedback.

Where we start

A bench is a fixed set of speakers and a fixed set of phrases, so that when two scoring methods disagree, the only thing that differed was the method. The speakers here are synthetic: each is a hum whose pitch follows the right tone shape, at a voice height and range inside the usual adult ranges, not a recording of a person. Part 1 · covers pitch tracking and contour scoring

How a machine hears a tone

6 min live

The tracker does five things, in order. It slices the sound into short windows, finds the pitch in each window, groups the voiced windows into syllables, turns each syllable into a shape on Chao's five-level scale, and picks the tone whose shape is nearest. Only the second step needs real mathematics, and it is a published method called YIN.

LiveWhat YIN does, in one paragraph3 min▶

A voiced sound repeats itself: the wave that leaves your throat on a steady pitch has the same shape every few milliseconds. YIN, published by de Cheveigné and Kawahara in 2002, slides a copy of each short window against itself and looks for the smallest delay at which the copy lines up with the original. That delay is one period of the voice; one divided by it is the pitch. A window with no clear repeat, such as a breath or the hiss of an s, has no pitch and is marked unvoiced, which is how the tracker finds where syllables begin and end.

Self-studyWhat the trainer cannot hear2 min▶

It scores pitch shape and nothing else. It cannot tell a q from a ch, an an from an ang, or whether a syllable was too long; it does not know which word you meant. A real voice also adds creak at the bottom of a third tone and small pitch bumps from consonants, which the synthetic bench voices do not have, so the bench claims only the ordering of the methods, never an accuracy for real speech. Use the trainer for tones, and a teacher or a native speaker for everything else.

Real world

"Your pitch is too low." A constructed case with a familiar shape. A Lumen engineer with a deep voice tried a free tone app that compared his pitch with a recorded female teacher's, in hertz. It marked nearly every fourth tone wrong because his "high" start was lower than her middle. He concluded he had a bad ear. His shapes were fine; the app was measuring height. The bench below counts what that method does to a lower voice.

LiveFinding 1: hertz punishes the lower and the narrower voice2 min▶

A method that compares raw pitch with one template voice is right for voices near that template and wrong for everyone else. On the bench it scores the higher voice 23 of 23 and the lower voice 6 of 23, though both said exactly the same shapes, and it catches only 7 of the lower voice's 12 real mistakes. Measuring each syllable in semitones from the speaker's own middle fixes the lower voice. Scaling to the speaker's own range as well fixes the narrow-range voice, the person who speaks with little pitch movement, who otherwise loses 4 tones.

LiveFinding 2: a word on one level needs your profile2 min▶

公司 (gōngsī, company) is two first tones: high and level, twice. Judged on its own, a phrase with no pitch movement has no range to measure, so the method cannot tell "high and level" from "middle and level", and both syllables are missed. Calibrating once, on mā má mǎ mà (the four tones on one syllable), gives the trainer your middle and your range, and every phrase after that is judged against them. It is the difference between 21 and 23, and it is why the practice tool asks for calibration first.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever

Scoring the dictionary tones marks fluent speech wrong

4 min live

The obvious way to build a checker is to look up each character's tone and score against it. The bench counts what that does: five syllables a native speaker says correctly, every first syllable of a tone change, are marked wrong, and the learner is pushed toward the careful, slightly foreign pronunciation of each character said alone.

LiveWhy the obvious checker is the wrong one2 min▶

A learner who is marked wrong for saying ní hǎo will try saying nǐ hǎo with two full third tones, which is harder to say and sounds stilted to a native listener. The checker's error becomes the learner's habit. The trainer applies the three tone-change rules from session 1 to the phrase first, then scores against the tones a native speaker actually produces; the five syllables the dictionary checker rejects are exactly the ones it accepts.

Self-studyThe honest limit: when dictionary tones are what you want2 min▶

Learning a new word, you need its dictionary tone: that is what you look up, and it is what you will see written in standard pinyin. The trainer's practice list shows both, the dictionary tones and the tones as said, for every phrase. The rule is simple: learn the word by its dictionary tone, say the phrase by its spoken tone, and score the phrase by the spoken tone.

Real world

The careful greeter. A constructed case. After a month with a dictionary-tone app, a Lumen manager greeted the Shanghai team with a slow, perfectly dipped nǐ, then hǎo. It was understood at once and sounded like a recording. Nobody corrected him; the Shanghai side switched to English, which is the quiet signal that a learner sounds effortful. One evening on the surface rules, and his 你好 (nǐ hǎo, hello) sounded like everyone else's.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Calibrate, then say the twelve words on three different days. Write the score each day and the one tone you miss most.
  2. Drill your weakest tone in pairs: if it is the third, alternate 买 (mǎi, buy) and 卖 (mài, sell); if the second, 十 (shí, ten) against 是 (shì, to be, yes).
  3. Read the five tone changes aloud until the changed tone comes without thinking: ní hǎo, láobǎn, bú shì, yídìng, yìqǐ.
  4. Bring a price and a date from your own work. Session 4 is numbers, money, dates and times, where tone mistakes become wrong quantities.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A tone app compares your pitch in hertz with a recorded teacher's. Your voice is much lower than hers. What does the bench predict?

Tones are shapes relative to your own voice. A method that measures height against one template voice confuses "a lower voice" with "a lower tone", which is why "your pitch is too low" is never useful tone feedback.

2 · Why does the trainer ask you to say mā má mǎ mà before anything else?

Two first tones are level and high; with nothing else to compare against, "high" cannot be told from "middle". Your calibration is that comparison. Nothing is uploaded: the recording stays in the page.

3 · A checker scores every phrase against the dictionary tone of each character. What goes wrong?

Learn a word by its dictionary tone; score a phrase by its spoken tone. The trainer applies the three rules first and shows you both, so you see the change rather than being marked down for making it.
